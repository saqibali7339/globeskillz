Two cities, one search market: the capital’s double economy

Edinburgh is the UK’s second most attractive city for foreign direct investment — behind only London, ahead of Manchester and Birmingham, according to the Centre for Economics and Business Research’s 2026 Investment Confidence Index. It’s a global fund-management hub: Baillie Gifford, Standard Life, Scottish Widows, and the Aberdeen Group are headquartered here, alongside major operations for BlackRock, Citi, State Street, and HSBC. Around 35,000 people work in financial services in the city. For B2B firms, that means keywords like “financial adviser Edinburgh” or “wealth management Edinburgh” carry serious commercial weight — and attract competition from London firms targeting the capital from 400 miles away.

Then, every August, the city becomes the world’s largest stage. The Edinburgh Festival Fringe — the biggest performing arts festival on earth — issued more than 2.8 million tickets in 2026, up 8% on 2025, across thousands of shows and hundreds of venues, with the International Festival, Book Festival, Film Festival, and Art Festival running alongside it. Add Hogmanay in December and the Christmas markets, and Edinburgh has the most extreme seasonal demand curve in UK local search. Hotels, restaurants, bars, attractions, and transport firms don’t have one trading year — they have a year-round baseline plus two or three compressed gold rushes.

For a local business, that duality is the whole strategy question. A solicitor in the New Town needs durable, authority-driven rankings that compound quietly all year. A restaurant on the Royal Mile needs to peak violently in August and December without going dark in between. Most SEO providers pick one game. I build for both — a steady authority engine underneath, and a seasonal engine on top that treats every festival as a campaign with a before, during, and after.

The August economy

Planning SEO around the Fringe changes everything

Here’s the mistake most Edinburgh hospitality businesses make: they treat August as something that happens to them. The Fringe programme is effectively announced months in advance, accommodation books out early, and visitor searches — “hotels Edinburgh August”, “restaurants near Royal Mile”, “things to do Edinburgh” — start climbing in spring. Businesses that publish seasonal content in April and May, keep their Business Profiles event-ready, and run review pushes through August capture that wave. Businesses that don’t, watch it break over their competitors.

The mechanics are straightforward. Seasonal landing pages (“where to eat near the Fringe”, “Fringe accommodation guide”) published early earn links and rankings before demand peaks. Google Business Profile posts through July and August keep you visible in the map pack when visitor searches spike. And August visitors are a review goldmine — tourists review at far higher rates than locals, so a systematic ask during the festival builds a review profile that keeps working in February. Each festival season should outperform the last; that’s a measurable target, and I report against it.

The same logic applies to Hogmanay and the Christmas markets, on a shorter fuse. And it applies in reverse to B2B: January’s financial year-end drives a surge in accountant and adviser searches, while September’s student return spikes lettings and storage. Edinburgh’s sectors and how they search

Beneath the seasons, the city’s sector geography matters. Professional services cluster in the New Town and West End, where searches are high-value and reputation-led. Hospitality concentrates on the Royal Mile, Grassmarket, and Old Town, where searches are tourist-heavy and review-led. Independent retail and food have their strongholds in Stockbridge, Bruntsfield, Morningside, and Leith’s Shore — each with a distinct local identity that shows up in how people search.

The capital problem

Why Edinburgh SEO is not Glasgow SEO

Agencies sell Edinburgh and Glasgow the same “Scottish city” package. It fails in both, for opposite reasons. Glasgow’s fight is neighbourhood density — a dozen micro-markets in one city. Edinburgh’s fight is duality and distance: half your competitors are local, and half are London firms buying their way into capital-city keywords. Ranking “wealth management Edinburgh” means out-writing and out-linking national brands, not just the firm on the next street.

Then there’s the tourist-resident split, which quietly breaks most keyword strategies. Searches containing “Royal Mile”, “Princes Street”, or “castle view” are overwhelmingly tourists — different intent, different conversion behaviour, different review expectations than a Morningside resident searching for a plumber. If your content doesn’t distinguish the two audiences, you’ll rank for traffic that never converts and miss the searches that do.

And Edinburgh’s neighbourhoods have sharper identities than most cities’ — Leith is not Stockbridge is not Morningside, and residents search accordingly. “Coffee shop Leith” and “coffee shop Stockbridge” are different searches with different winners. Hyperlocal pages that respect those identities — written like someone who knows the difference between the Shore and Broughton Street — beat generic “Edinburgh” pages every time. That local fluency is the edge a national agency can’t fake.

Two audiences

Tourists and residents search differently — write for both

This is the subtle mistake that costs Edinburgh businesses more traffic than any technical error: writing for one audience when two are searching. A tourist typing “best restaurants Royal Mile” wants reassurance, photos, and a bookable table within walking distance of their hotel. A Morningside resident typing “restaurants near me” on a Friday wants their local shortlist, opening hours, and whether they need to book. Same city, same service, completely different intent — and Google serves completely different results.

The fix is to separate the tracks on your site. Visitor-facing pages lean into landmarks, itineraries, and reassurance: where you are relative to the Castle, the Mile, or Waverley; what tourists ask (bookings, dietary options, group sizes); reviews from visitors front and centre. Resident-facing pages lean into neighbourhood identity and practicality: the area you serve, your regulars, your opening hours, your delivery radius. When one page tries to do both jobs, it converts neither audience well.

Your Google Business Profile has to serve both too, and here the seasonal rhythm matters. In July and August, visitor-oriented posts (“Fringe pre-show menu”, “walk-ins welcome”) and fresh photos of a buzzing room win the tourist map pack. In winter, the same profile should speak to locals: regular offers, community events, the quiet-season reasons to visit. Businesses that set their profile once and forget it are effectively marketing to August in February — and wondering why the phone’s quiet.

  • Visitor pages — landmarks, reassurance, bookability; reviews from tourists highlighted.
  • Resident pages — neighbourhood identity, practicality, loyalty; reviews from locals highlighted.
  • Seasonal profile shifts — posts and photos that match who’s actually searching this month.
  • Q&A as FAQ — seed your Business Profile Q&A with the questions each audience really asks.

Edinburgh businesses that nail this duality get something rare: a website that peaks with the festivals and holds its baseline through winter. Most competitors pick one audience by accident — usually whichever they thought of first. Serving both deliberately is a structural advantage that compounds every single season.
