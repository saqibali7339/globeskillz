The valley is one search market, not six

Rossendale is one of Lancashire’s smallest boroughs — around 70,000 people strung along the deeply incised upper valley of the River Irwell, between the conurbations of Greater Manchester and West Yorkshire. A string of former mill towns — Rawtenstall, Bacup, and Haslingden — runs east to west along the narrow valley floor, flanked by Pennine moorland. Eighty-eight per cent of the borough is green space. It’s a beautiful place to live, and a distinctive place to do SEO.

Here’s the insight that shapes everything: Google treats the valley as a single market. The map pack for “plumber Rossendale” or “roofer Rossendale” draws from Rawtenstall to Bacup in one result set — there aren’t separate packs per town the way there are per neighbourhood in Manchester. That’s enormously good news for a local business, because it means one well-built presence can dominate the entire valley. You don’t need city-scale budgets or a dozen location pages. You need the fundamentals done better than anyone else in the valley — and frankly, the bar isn’t high.

The valley’s economy tells you where the searches are. Trades and home services dominate — the stone terraces and converted mills need constant upkeep, and every town has its roofers, plumbers, electricians, and builders competing for the same valley-wide searches. Rawtenstall, the largest town, keeps going from strength to strength with its market, independent shops, and visitor draws like the East Lancashire Railway’s steam terminus and Ski Rossendale. Haslingden has a growing food-and-drink scene, with former shops on Deardengate converting to bars. Bacup, with its Victorian conservation-area centre, is still working through the high-street transition — the council’s own plan says it needs to transform to meet changing needs. Across the borough, empty shops ran at around 22% in 2023–24. For visible high-street businesses, winning online isn’t optional anymore — it’s how you survive the transition.

The valley keyword question

“Rossendale” or “Rawtenstall”? The question every valley business asks

This is the single most common question I get from valley businesses, and the answer is: both, but for different jobs. “Rossendale” keywords (“plumber Rossendale”, “florist Rossendale”) carry the biggest valley-wide volume — they’re what people type when they want someone who covers the area. Town keywords (“plumber Rawtenstall”, “electrician Bacup”) carry less volume but sharper intent — the searcher has a postcode in mind. Your homepage and main service pages target the valley terms; supporting pages or sections pick up the town terms. It’s not either-or; it’s architecture.

There’s a second question hiding behind the first: can a Haslingden business rank for Bacup searches? Yes — because it’s one market. Google’s local results for valley trades routinely mix businesses from all three towns, ranked on relevance, reviews, and profile strength rather than which town the searcher named. A Haslingden roofer with 120 reviews and a complete profile will outrank a Bacup roofer with 12 reviews for “roofer Bacup”. Proximity helps at the margins, but in a valley this compact, strength beats postcode.

Small-market SEO

What actually moves rankings in a market this size

In a city, rankings are a knife fight between dozens of optimised competitors. In Rossendale, they’re usually decided by who bothered. I audit valley businesses every month, and the pattern barely varies: unclaimed or half-finished Google Business Profiles, a dozen reviews gathered by accident over five years, citation listings that disagree about the address, and a website that hasn’t been updated since 2019 — if there’s a website at all, rather than a Facebook page doing all the work.

Facebook deserves an honest paragraph, because in the valley it’s genuinely powerful — community groups drive real enquiries. But Facebook doesn’t rank on Google, and Google is where new customers search when they don’t already know your name. “Plumber Rossendale” isn’t typed into Facebook. The businesses winning the valley do both: Facebook for community, Google for capture. One without the other leaves money on the table.

Because competition is thin, the ranking factors stack differently here than in a city. Reviews and profile completeness carry enormous weight — they’re often the entire difference between position one and position six. Backlinks, which dominate city SEO budgets, matter far less; a handful of genuine valley links (local press, community organisations, suppliers) is plenty. Beyond the valley

The commuter bonus: searches from outside Rossendale

Here’s a source of business most valley firms never think to target: the commuter belt. The M66 connects the western end of the valley directly to the motorway network, and every weekday thousands of people drive between Rossendale and Manchester, Bury, and Rochdale. Those commuters search from both ends — “near me” from the office in Manchester, valley terms from the sofa in Haslingden. If your service areas and content only mention the valley, you’re invisible to half your potential catchment.

The fix is simple and honest: state where you actually work. “Covering Rossendale, Bury, Rochdale, and surrounding areas” on your website and in your Google Business Profile service areas turns passing trade into booked work — no trickery, just accuracy. I’ve seen valley trades businesses pick up a steady stream of Bury and Rochdale jobs purely because they were the only local firm whose profile said they covered it. Your competitors’ silence is your opportunity.

There’s a second outside audience: visitors. Ski Rossendale, the East Lancashire Railway, the Halo, and the valley’s walking country pull day-trippers from across the North West — and day-trippers search “cafes near Rawtenstall”, “pubs near Haslingden”, “things to do Rossendale” from their phones. Hospitality and retail businesses near the attractions should treat visitor searches as a distinct, winnable segment: a complete profile, visitor-friendly photos, and reviews mentioning the attraction nearby.

  • Declare your real service area — valley plus Bury, Rochdale, and anywhere else you genuinely cover.
  • Commuter-hour visibility — “near me” searches peak on mobile; your profile must be flawless there.
  • Visitor content — pages and posts tied to the railway, the ski slope, the Halo, and the walks.
  • Reviews that mention place — “came up from Bury, brilliant service” helps you rank beyond the valley.

The valley will always be your core market — but the businesses that grow fastest here are the ones that treat the M66 as an asset, not a boundary. One well-set service area, honestly declared, can add a whole second catchment to your enquiry flow.
