Devon is a county, not a city — your SEO has to act like it.

Most location SEO is built for cities. Devon breaks that model completely. This is a county of four distinct economies spread across two coastlines, a cathedral city, a naval city, a world-famous seaside riviera and hundreds of villages — and a business in Barnstaple shares almost nothing with a business in Torquay except the county name. Targeting "Devon" as if it were one market is the single most common mistake I see on Devon business websites.

The county's economy tells the story. The visitor economy is worth £1.6 billion a year and supports over 50,000 jobs — roughly 9% of all employment in Devon. Farming is a bedrock sector: Devon is home to around 30% of the UK's dairy industry, and Brixham lands one of the most valuable catches of any fishing port in the country. Underneath sits a small-and-micro-business economy — Devon's business base is overwhelmingly SMEs, which makes it resilient but also means most businesses are doing their own marketing badly, on top of everything else.

For SEO, that fragmentation is actually an opportunity. Competition for "Devon" terms is real, but competition for "Brixham fish restaurant", "wedding venue South Hams" or "holiday cottage North Devon" is winnable — and those specific searches convert far better than generic county terms. The businesses winning in Devon aren't the ones targeting the biggest keywords; they're the ones owning their patch.

Three playbooks

Exeter, Plymouth, Torbay: three different playbooks.

Exeter is Devon's year-round engine. The cathedral city hosts the University of Exeter, Exeter Science Park's growing tech cluster, the Met Office and Pennon Group — plus a professional-services sector (solicitors, land agents, accountants) that serves the whole of rural Devon. Princesshay and the Guildhall area anchor one of the South West's strongest independent retail and hospitality scenes. Search here is steady across the year: professional intent, student intent and affluent resident intent, all overlapping. It's also the most competitive market in the county — every agency in the South West targets "Exeter" keywords. Winning here takes sustained authority: expertise content, deep review profiles and real local links.

Plymouth plays a different game. HMNB Devonport — the largest naval base in Western Europe — anchors a marine, defence and engineering supply chain that most SEO advice completely ignores. B2B searches like "marine engineering Plymouth" or "defence contractor South West" are low-volume but enormously valuable, and the competition is thin because consumer-focused agencies don't understand the buyer. On the consumer side, the Barbican, the Hoe and Drake Circus drive hospitality and retail searches with a strong local-identity flavour — Plymothians are loyal, and reviews from local voices carry weight.

Torbay — Torquay, Paignton and Brixham, the English Riviera — is the seasonal extreme. In summer it's one of Britain's most popular seaside destinations: hotels, guest houses, holiday parks, restaurants, attractions and a huge holiday-let market all competing for visitor searches. In winter, demand collapses back to the local population. Brixham adds its own layer: one of the UK's most productive fishing ports by value of catch, with a working waterfront economy underneath the tourism. The winning move in Torbay is calendar-driven SEO — summer content published in spring, winter content (locals' offers, off-season breaks, Christmas) that keeps revenue alive when the visitors leave.

And then there's rural Devon — the South Hams, North Devon, the Dartmoor fringe. Farm shops, holiday cottages, artisan food producers, wedding venues in barns, outdoor activity firms. Search volumes are small, but intent is extremely high and competition is minimal. A farm shop near Totnes that builds a proper Google profile and a few village-specific pages can dominate its patch within months. This is the lowest-hanging fruit in the entire county.

SEO in Devon is a calendar discipline. The businesses that treat it that way get two peak seasons a year; everyone else gets whatever's left.

Multi-location strategy

The multi-location playbook that actually works in Devon.

Plenty of Devon businesses serve the whole county — trades firms, distributors, agencies, care providers. The temptation is one website, one "areas we cover" page listing fifty towns. Google reads that page as being about nothing in particular, and it ranks for nothing in particular.

The playbook that works:

  • One real page per real market. Not fifty thin town pages — five to eight substantial ones: Exeter, Plymouth, Torquay, Barnstaple, Exmouth, Totnes/South Hams, Tiverton/Mid Devon. Each with genuine local proof: jobs done there, local photos, local testimonials, parking and access notes.
  • One Google Business Profile per genuine location. A profile for every town you don't actually operate from is a suspension waiting to happen. Service-area businesses get one profile with service areas set honestly — then win with reviews and content, not fake pins.
  • County-level hub, town-level spokes. A strong "our Devon coverage" hub page linking to each market page builds topical authority for "Devon" terms while each spoke fights its local battle. This is how you rank for both "SEO Devon" and "SEO Exeter" without cannibalising yourself.
  • Local citations that match the patch. Devon-specific directories, local press, parish and town council business listings — these carry local-relevance weight that national directories can't.
  • Reviews that name the place. "Great service in our Barnstaple office" in a review is a local-relevance signal. Encourage customers to mention where the work happened.

For single-location businesses the advice inverts: own your patch completely before expanding. A Brixham restaurant that dominates Brixham searches earns more than one spread thin across "Devon seafood". Depth beats breadth until depth is won.

Reviews

Reviews with a season attached.

Reviews decide the Devon map pack — and in a seasonal county, review recency matters more than anywhere else. A Torquay hotel with forty glowing reviews from last August and nothing since looks dormant to both Google and the February booker planning a summer trip. A Brixham restaurant whose latest review is from October looks closed for winter, even if it's open.

The fix is a review engine tuned to the season: ask every happy customer, every time, with a one-tap link — and in the quiet months, actively generate the reviews that keep the profile alive. Winter diners, off-season hotel guests, locals' events: these are review opportunities most Devon businesses waste. A steady twelve-month flow of reviews beats a summer spike followed by silence, because it tells Google (correctly) that you're a going concern, not a seasonal pop-up.

Respond to every review within 48 hours, and mention the place naturally in responses: "Glad you enjoyed the harbour view — see you next time you're in Brixham." It costs nothing, it reads as genuine local pride, and it reinforces exactly the local signals Google is looking for.

Market towns

Don't forget the market towns.

Exeter, Plymouth and Torbay get the attention, but Devon's market towns — Barnstaple, Tiverton, Totnes, Tavistock, Okehampton, Honiton, Newton Abbot — are where some of the best SEO value in the county sits. Each is the commercial centre for a wide rural hinterland: people drive in from a dozen villages for everything from solicitors to sofa shops. Search volumes are modest, but the buyer intent is fierce and the competition is usually one established firm coasting on reputation.

Totnes deserves special mention: its alternative, independent character creates searches no keyword tool predicts well — "refill shop Totnes", "holistic therapist South Hams" — and a fiercely loyal local audience that reviews generously. Tavistock, a UNESCO World Heritage Site town on the edge of Dartmoor, pulls walker and visitor searches year-round. Barnstaple serves the whole of North Devon. A business that builds genuine pages for two or three of these towns — with real local proof, not template text — can build a county-wide footprint one winnable battle at a time.
