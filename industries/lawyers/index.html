An uncomfortable starting point

Your Website Was Built to Impress Other Lawyers

Walk through most law firm websites and you will notice something odd. The gavels, the columns, the Latin phrases, the long list of landmark cases. They are designed to impress other lawyers. There is just one problem: other lawyers are not the ones hiring you. A person facing a divorce, a criminal charge, an injury, or a business dispute does not choose counsel the way a peer would evaluate it. They choose the firm that feels competent, honest, and reachable at the worst moment of their year.

That gap, between the brochure your partners approved and the reassurance a frightened client needs, is where most law firm marketing quietly fails. The site talks about the firm. The client is asking about themselves: what happens if I call, what will this cost me, will this person actually understand my situation, and can I trust them with it. Every page I build is organized around those four questions, because the firms that answer them plainly are the firms that get the consultation.

Google rewards the same thing clients reward. Legal content sits under the strictest quality standards in search, and the pages that rank are the ones that genuinely serve the searcher. A worried person comparing three firms at midnight is the most honest judge of usefulness there is. Build for them and the rankings follow as a side effect. Build for your partners' egos and you get a beautiful site nobody calls.

Page architecture

One Page Per Practice Area, Built Like a Consultation

A single practice areas page listing everything you do cannot rank for anything specific, and it cannot reassure anyone either. I build one substantial page per practice area, each written around how clients actually search and what they need to hear: divorce, personal injury, criminal defense, business disputes, each with its own intent, its own worries, its own language. Every page mirrors a first consultation in structure, because that is what the searcher is really looking for.

What goes on each page: who the page is for, stated plainly in the first paragraph. The process from first call to resolution, step by step, in plain language. Honest guidance on fees and costs wherever your jurisdiction's rules allow it, because cost is the question everyone asks and almost no firm answers. The real questions your intake team hears every week, answered properly. And the name of the attorney behind it, because clients hire people, not firms. Attorney bio pages get the same depth: credentials, background, approach, areas of focus, written to read like a person rather than a resume. Bio pages are among the most visited pages on any law firm site, and most firms waste them on three generic sentences.

Underneath, each page gets proper technical structure: headings that match search intent, Attorney and LegalService schema so Google understands exactly who you are, FAQ schema on the genuine questions, and internal links between related practice areas so authority flows where it helps most. For firms serving multiple cities, location variants are built only where you genuinely practice, with real local substance, never find-and-replace templates. Depth in each practice area beats breadth across the site. That is the whole architecture, and it is why thin firm sites lose to deeper ones even when the deeper firm is smaller.

The rules

Marketing That Stays Inside the Ethics Rules

This deserves its own section, because it is where most legal marketing goes wrong. Bar advertising rules vary by jurisdiction, and they constrain claims, testimonials, comparisons, and solicitation. I stay clear of all of it: no guaranteed outcomes, no superlatives about your firm, no fabricated reviews, no content that overstates what you do or whom you have helped.

What we do instead is simple and defensible. Accurate information about your services. Real credentials, plainly stated. Genuine client feedback, handled within the rules. Educational content that helps people understand their situation before they ever call. This is slower than hype, but it compounds, and it never puts your reputation or your license at risk.

If you ever wonder whether something crosses a line, ask me and I will tell you straight. I would rather talk you out of a risky tactic than implement it. The firms I work with are building practices for decades. The marketing should match that horizon. The same trust-first discipline applies to the professional services practices I work with, where credibility is the product too.

Authority, the defensible way

Links You Could Show a Journalist

Law firms need links, but the usual link schemes would embarrass you if anyone looked closely, and in legal, people do look closely. So I build authority the slow way: genuine guides and explainers that earn citations because they are actually useful. A clear, accurate guide to a legal process in your jurisdiction gets referenced by local press, community sites, and organizations. Nobody has to be paid and nothing has to be hidden.

Your directory footprint gets the same care: complete, consistent profiles across the legal directories that matter, because they feed both visibility and the trust signals a comparing client checks. Bar association mentions, speaking engagements, community involvement, all the real-world credibility your firm already has, made visible online where it counts. Real authority exists before the website. My job is to make sure Google and your future clients can see it.

The intake gap

Where Firms Lose the Clients SEO Brings Them

Here is something agencies rarely mention: a meaningful share of legal enquiries dies between the form submission and the callback. The client who found you at midnight and filled in your form at 12:40am will call the second firm on their list by 9am if nobody responds. SEO can only bring the enquiry to your door. What happens in the first hour decides whether it becomes a consultation.

So part of my work is looking at your intake with fresh eyes. Is the phone number visible on every page, on mobile, without scrolling. Does the contact form ask for three fields or thirteen. Is there a clear statement of what happens after someone reaches out, and how fast. Do you have a system for after-hours enquiries, even if it is just a well-set expectation. These are not SEO tasks in the narrow sense, but they decide what your SEO is worth, so I treat them as part of the job.

The firms that win are not always the best ranked. They are the best ranked among the firms that answer quickly and make the first conversation easy. I will help you be both.

How we work

What the First 90 Days Look Like

Week one is the audit: your site, your profiles, your reviews, and the competitors outranking you, taken apart honestly. You get a plain-language report of what is working, what is broken, and what I would fix first. No jargon, no hundred-page PDF designed to justify a retainer.

Then we rebuild in order of impact. Practice-area pages get rewritten or built from scratch, with your approval on every word. Attorney bios get the depth they deserve. Your profiles across legal directories get completed and made consistent. A compliant review system goes in place. Technical issues, the slow pages, the broken structure, the missing schema, get fixed quietly in the background.

Every month you get a report showing what changed and what it produced: rankings, calls, form submissions. If something is not working, I tell you that too, and we change it. Legal SEO takes time, usually six to twelve months in a competitive market, and I will not pretend otherwise. What I promise is that you will always know exactly what your money is doing.
