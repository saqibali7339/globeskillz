Ecommerce SEO · the 9:47pm cart

It Is 9:47pm and Your Checkout Page Is Full

A customer has three of your products in her cart. She found you through Instagram three weeks ago, saved a post, forgot about it. Tonight she typed the product name into Google, and the store that showed up first was not yours. She bought from them. Your cart is still full. Their order confirmation is in her inbox.

That is the whole game of e-commerce SEO services in one scene. Social media rents you attention. Google captures demand at the exact moment someone is ready to buy. The searches are brutally commercial: best running shoes for flat feet, Dyson V15 vs V12, linen duvet cover king. The buyer has her card out. The only question is whose store Google shows her.

Most store owners I meet have this backwards. They treat their store as a catalog that happens to have a website, then wonder why a competitor with worse products outranks them. Google does not rank catalogs. It ranks pages that answer buyer searches better than anyone else. That means your product pages, your collection pages, and the guides around them are not decoration. They are the store.

I build e-commerce SEO the way a store owner would if they had the time: product pages that rank for what buyers actually type, collections that read like buying guides instead of filler, and content that catches people while they are still deciding. Whether your store runs on Shopify or WooCommerce, and whether you sell in the USA, the UK, or Canada, the buyers search the same way. My job is to make sure they find you.

Shopify vs WooCommerce

Your Platform Is Not Your Strategy

The first question almost every store owner asks me is which platform is better for SEO. Here is the honest answer: both can win, and both lose for completely different reasons. Shopify stores fight the platform's limits. WooCommerce stores fight their own freedom. The playbook is different, but the goal is identical: pages that rank and convert.

Platform The SEO fight What wins
Shopify Fixed URL structures, duplicate product URLs, app bloat you cannot fully control Canonical discipline, lean apps, collections and product copy that outwork the platform limits
WooCommerce Total freedom that turns into plugin bloat, thin archives, and slow themes A tight stack, real category copy, and someone who actually uses the control the platform gives you
Shopify

Shopify Makes Launching Easy and Ranking Hard

A store owner showed me her Shopify site once, proud as she should have been. Beautiful theme, two hundred products, decent Instagram following. And almost no traffic from Google. She had done everything Shopify told her to do: launched fast, added products, connected the apps. What nobody told her is that ranking a Shopify store is a separate craft from running one, and the platform does not do it for you.

The problems are platform-specific and invisible to most owners. Shopify auto-generates URL structures that create duplicate product and collection URLs, and without canonical control you are splitting your own rankings. Every app you installed adds weight, and most stores I audit are carrying apps they stopped using months ago, each one slowing every page. Then there is the copy problem: if your product descriptions came from the supplier, Google has already read them on ten other stores and has no reason to rank yours.

Where Shopify stores actually win is the collection page. Collections are your category powerhouses, and most stores waste them on a title and two sentences of filler. I rebuild yours around buyer intent: real copy instead of filler, smart internal linking that pushes authority to your bestsellers, and product pages with unique descriptions and proper schema so your listings can show prices, ratings, and stock right in the search results. A collection that reads like a buying guide outranks a thin one every time.

  • Duplicate product and collection URLs get canonical control instead of splitting your rankings.
  • App bloat gets audited app by app, with load-time numbers before anything is touched.
  • Supplier-pasted descriptions get rewritten with unique detail and the phrases buyers search.
  • Thin collections become buying guides with internal links that feed your bestsellers.
WooCommerce

WooCommerce Gives You the Keys. Most Stores Never Turn Them.

WooCommerce gives you total control over your store. You own the URLs, the schema, the speed stack, the content, everything a closed platform rations out. That is exactly why so many WooCommerce stores underperform on Google: control without direction turns into bloat. A plugin for everything. A theme chosen for its demo. Images never compressed. Category pages with no copy at all.

My audit starts under the hood, because content cannot rank on a site Google struggles to crawl. Twenty-plus active plugins, half of them redundant, several loading scripts on pages where they do nothing. Tag pages, attribute archives, and empty categories creating dozens of thin duplicate pages that dilute your rankings. Cart, checkout, and account pages sitting in the index where they should never be. Page-builder themes that look great in the demo and cost you seconds on every load.

I have built on WordPress for years, so this layer is familiar ground: hosting and caching fixed first, plugins mapped and cut on staging with your approval, thin archives noindexed or consolidated, and schema set up once, cleanly, instead of three plugins fighting over it. With the foundation solid, category pages get genuine buying-guide copy and product pages get titles shaped around real buyer searches. If your store runs on WordPress, you already own the best SEO platform in ecommerce. My job is to make it act like one.

  • Plugin bloat gets mapped plugin by plugin, with staging tests before anything changes on your live store.
  • Thin tag, attribute, and empty category archives get noindexed or consolidated so they stop diluting rankings.
  • Heavy page-builder themes get measured honestly, with numbers instead of rebuild pressure.
  • Schema gets one clean setup so products can show prices, ratings, and stock in search results.
The shared engine

Underneath Every Platform, the Same Three Things Do the Selling

Strip away the platform arguments and e-commerce SEO is the same three jobs everywhere. First, the money pages: product and collection pages shaped around the exact phrases buyers type, with the detail that makes each page unique instead of supplier copy. A product page that ranks but does not convert is a missed sale, so the SEO and the selling get built together.

Second, the deciding content. Buyers search questions your products answer: comparisons, best-of lists, how-to guides. A small set of buying guides tied to your collections often outperforms a big unfocused blog, and it funnels deciding buyers straight to your products instead of a competitor's. This is also where honest authority gets built, through content worth citing, not schemes.

Third, the invisible layer: speed, crawlability, and schema. Faster stores rank better and convert better, so speed work pays twice. Faceted navigation gets tamed so it stops spawning crawl waste. Product schema goes on every page so your listings stand out in the results. Do these three well and the store compounds: technical fixes pay off fastest, content and authority build month after month.

Honest mistakes

What Most Agencies Get Wrong About E-commerce SEO

They treat your store like a blog. Generalist SEO agencies sell the same package to everyone: some articles, some links, a report. A store does not need articles. It needs product pages that rank, collections that convert, and a technical foundation that lets Google crawl ten thousand SKUs without choking. If an agency's first suggestion for your store is "start a blog," they have never ranked a store.

They chase traffic instead of buyers. Ranking for "what is cashmere" brings readers. Ranking for "cashmere sweater womens sale" brings revenue. E-commerce keyword strategy lives in the commercial end of the search: exact products, comparisons, best-of terms, and the long tail of specific buyer questions. Vanity traffic looks great in a report and does nothing for your revenue.

They ignore the boring technical work. Faceted navigation spawning thousands of thin URLs. Variant pages competing with each other. A checkout flow leaking into the index. None of it is exciting, and all of it decides whether your money pages ever get the crawl attention they deserve. I do the boring work first, because the exciting work does not rank without it.

They never touch the product page. This is the big one. Most SEO engagements never rewrite a single product description, never fix a collection, never look at the checkout. But the product page is where the sale happens. My work ends at the money pages, not in a spreadsheet of rankings.
