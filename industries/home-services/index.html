Saturday morning

8:12am, and Somebody’s Day Just Broke

It is Saturday, 8:12am. A homeowner walks into the garage, hits the opener, and hears the bang: a snapped spring, a door that will not budge, a car trapped inside, and a kid’s football match at ten. Phone in one hand, they search "garage door spring repair near me" with the other. Three map results. They tap the one with two hundred recent reviews and a call button they can hit without scrolling. Job booked by 8:20.

Across town, another homeowner stares at a lawn that has gone feral and searches "lawn care service". They scroll straight past the company with no photos and a website from 2014. Two searches, two winners, one lesson: in home services, Google is the front door, the receptionist, and the word-of-mouth referral all at once.

I do SEO for home service businesses. Eleven trades, one playbook adapted eleven ways: be visible at the exact moment of need, look like the safe choice within seconds, and make calling effortless. A locksmith at 2am and a landscaper selling a $15,000 patio do not share a strategy, but they share that sentence. Below is how it plays out per trade, condensed from the full playbooks I used to run as standalone pages.

Emergency trades

Locked Out at 2am

It is 2am, raining, and someone is standing outside their own front door with no keys and eight percent battery. They search "locksmith near me" and call the first result that looks legitimate. No comparing quotes, no about page. Speed and trust, decided in seconds. I optimize for that moment: map pack ranking, reviews visible without scrolling, a tappable number, a name that sounds like a real local company.

This trade has a complication most do not: the results are full of fakes. Generic names, stock photos, review bursts posted in the same week. Beating them is not about tricks. It is about being unmistakably real: a verified profile, real photos of real vans and people, reviews that sound like actual customers.

  • At 2am the map pack, the reviews, and the call button matter more than your entire website.
  • A real local name and real photos separate you from the lead-gen listings above you.
  • Reviews mentioning fast arrival and fair pricing do the selling while you sleep.
Emergency trades

When the Spring Snaps at 7am

A garage door company is two businesses sharing one phone number. Emergency repairs: springs, cables, openers that died overnight, decided in minutes. And replacements: the full new system, the thousand-dollar-plus sale researched over weeks. Same company, different customers, different searches. Most sites serve only one well, usually neither.

So I build both funnels. Repair pages lead with availability: broken spring, cable, and opener repair, each opening with same-day service and a tap-to-call button. Replacement pages lead with proof: style galleries and cost guidance. One van in the USA or a fleet across the UK, the split is the same.

  • Repair pages lead with availability; replacement pages lead with proof.
  • "Broken spring" searches are pure emergency gold: decided in minutes.
  • The customer calls two or three companies. Whoever picks up first usually wins.
Trust trades

You Are Selling Trust, Not Mopping

A cleaning customer is buying one thing: the feeling that letting a stranger into their home is safe. The mop is irrelevant. Every search and every review read is really one question: can I trust these people in my home.

So the strategy answers the trust question before it is asked. Background checks, explained. Insured and bonded, with what it covers. The same team every visit. Reviews that say "I trust them with my keys". Get trust right and the model follows: recurring plans, the most valuable customer in home services, sold on autopilot.

  • Trust language woven through the whole site beats badges slapped in a footer.
  • Reviews mentioning keys, kids, and pets convert better than generic praise.
  • Recurring plans are the prize; every page should sell the subscription, not the one-off.
Trust trades

The Red Wine Saturday Night

Saturday night, red wine meets a cream carpet. Sunday morning: "red wine stain carpet cleaning", "pet urine removal service". Nobody plans these searches. They happen fast, on phones, and go to whoever looks capable of fixing the disaster. "Carpet cleaning near me" might be browsing; "how to get dog urine out of carpet" is hiring today.

Pages get built around the stain and the proof, with before-and-after photos doing the pitching. Underneath the emergencies sits the steadiest customer in the business: the landlord. Turnover cleans between tenants, recurring and predictable, won by whoever targets property managers while competitors chase homeowners.

  • Stain-specific pages outrank generic "carpet cleaning" pages for the searches that hire today.
  • Before-and-after photos are non-negotiable: the photo is the proof and the pitch in one.
  • Landlord and property-manager content unlocks recurring turnover work most cleaners ignore.
Seasonal trades

February Decides Your Whole Year

In February nobody thinks about their lawn, which is why February decides your whole year. The April searcher has already seen your competitor’s reviews and quote form for two months. You are not competing for the April search; you are competing for everything before it. I plan the year backwards from the surge.

Late winter is build season: service pages, town pages, timing guides published while nobody is buying, ranking when researchers appear. Then the fertilization spike hits before a single mower starts. And the product is never the mow. It is the route: the same lawns, every week. Every page sells the subscription.

  • Content published in winter ranks by the time spring researchers appear.
  • Sell the weekly route, not the single cut.
Seasonal trades

Spring Does Not Wait

Every March, homeowners see a winter of grime and search for someone to blast it away. The companies that show up first book solid for eight weeks. February is the deadline, not the starting line: rankings and reviews must be in place before the surge.

The prep list is short. A before-and-after gallery per surface, driveway, siding, deck, roof, with image names that rank. A profile with every service listed and spring hours accurate, plus a late-winter review push. One page per surface, because "driveway pressure washing" and "house siding washing" are different searches. Most competitors run a homepage and a phone number.

  • Gallery pages per surface rank on their own and sell the job visually.
  • A late-winter review push means entering March with fresh momentum.
  • Separate pages per surface: the intent behind each search is different.
Seasonal trades

The Route Is the Business

The money is the route: a storefront cleaned every two weeks, fifty weeks a year, beats a hundred one-off house jobs. Residential searches spike every spring, but commercial route keywords, "storefront window cleaning", "office window cleaning contract", are where a business becomes stable. Most cleaners never target them.

The work photographs beautifully: spotless versus grimy glass is an instant sale, and almost nobody here does image SEO on purpose. Two customers, two pages. The storefront owner wants reliability and a schedule; the homeowner wants sparkle before the holidays. I build for both, with route pages carrying the revenue story.

  • Commercial route keywords are the highest-value, least-contested targets in the trade.
  • Spotless-versus-grimy photos sell instantly; image SEO here is wide open.
Big-ticket trades

The $15,000 Patio Starts With a Search

Saturday morning, coffee in hand, a couple stares at a mud patch where a patio should be. They search "patio installation near me". Whoever earns their trust in the next twenty minutes probably gets a five-figure project. Landscaping has the highest job values in the outdoor trades; every ranking position is worth real money.

These buyers research for weeks and compare portfolios like art galleries. So I build project pages, not grids: one page per significant job with location, scope, materials, and muddy-to-reveal photos. Reviews must mention the project, not just the crew: design, communication, on budget. Most competitors fail this test, which is the opportunity.

  • Project pages with scope, materials, and start-to-finish photos beat gallery grids.
  • Reviews should mention design and communication, the words big-ticket buyers scan for.
  • The competitive bar is low: sharp photos and real project detail already stand out.
Big-ticket trades

Nobody Picks a Painter Off a Flyer Anymore

Nobody picks a painter off a flyer now. They search "house painters near me", open three sites, check photos and reviews, and request quotes from the two that look most professional. A considered purchase, researched over weeks, which is good news: the best content wins, not the biggest ad budget.

Cost guides and "how long does exterior paint last" capture buyers early and keep your name in front until the quote request. And the quote is the conversion. "We get visitors but no calls" almost always means the site fails at the decision moment: blurry gallery, no quote form, a phone number in the footer. Interior and exterior get separate pages.

  • Cost guides and timing content win the researcher weeks before the quote request.
  • Reviews must mention cleanliness and reliability, the two things homeowners fear most.
Big-ticket trades

The Customer Who Researches for Weeks

Siding is a five-figure decision made once or twice in a lifetime. Buyers spend weeks comparing vinyl versus fiber cement, worrying about installation quality, collecting three or four quotes. Every step happens on Google before you get a call. Your site must be the most helpful voice in that anxious research.

Honest material comparisons and current-year cost guidance win the quote request. And there is a structural gift: siding keywords are far less competitive than roofing terms, so one strong site can dominate a metro while roofers fight over scraps. Storm and hail damage pages catch the surge nobody plans for.

  • Honest material comparisons win the quote in a research-heavy purchase.
  • Siding terms are less contested than roofing: one strong site can own a metro.
  • Storm-damage content captures the surge your competitors never prepared for.
Speed trades

The 9pm Couch Problem

9pm Tuesday. Someone stares at a couch that must be gone before Thursday’s delivery. No research happens. They type "couch removal near me" and call the first company that looks real, available, and fast. Junk removal is triggered, not planned: moves, bereavements, renovations, landlord deadlines.

Nearly every valuable search carries same-day intent, usually on a phone, often from the room the junk sits in. The winner loads fastest, shows tap-to-call before any scrolling, and has reviews mentioning speed and fair pricing. The profile outranks the website here: the map pack is the whole decision. Speed, proof, availability. That is the trade.

  • Same-day and "today" searches carry the money; everything serves them.
  • Fastest site plus tap-to-call plus speed-mentioning reviews wins the stressed searcher.
  • The profile matters more than the website: the map pack is the decision.
Blunt section

The Same Five Mistakes on Almost Every Site I Audit

After auditing home services sites across these eleven trades, the failures repeat so reliably I can predict them before the audit loads. One generic services page trying to rank for everything, which ranks for nothing. Photos that are blurry, stock, or missing entirely, in businesses where the photo is the pitch. A review drought: twelve reviews from two years ago while the competitor adds five a month. A phone number buried in the footer on sites where the entire sale is a phone call. And service-area vagueness, either claiming everywhere with thin location pages or claiming nowhere at all.

None of these need a redesign to fix. They need someone to treat the website like what it is: a salesperson that works the phones while you work the jobs. Fix the five and most local competitors are already behind you, whether you clean carpets in Canada or paint houses in Australia.
