The 11pm call

Nobody plans a pest problem. I build for the panic.

It is 11pm and there is scratching in the walls. Or a tenant sends a photo nobody wants to receive. Nobody schedules a pest problem for next quarter, and nobody comparison-shops one the way they would a sofa. The customer is not browsing. They have a problem right now, they are slightly panicked, and they will hire whoever looks competent and available first. That single fact is the entire psychology of pest control search, and everything I build for this trade starts there.

The searches are brutally specific: bed bug exterminator near me, termite inspection cost, rat removal service. Notice what is missing: the words "pest control." The customer is not looking for pest control. They are looking for someone who kills bed bugs, today, in their area. Generic pages lose to treatment-specific pages every single time, because Google rewards the page that matches the exact fear behind the query, and the customer rewards it with the call.

And the decision happens on a phone, in minutes. The searcher scans the top map results, checks star ratings and review snippets, and taps call. Your website gets maybe sixty seconds of attention, most of it spent hunting for a phone number. That is why the business profile, the review flow, and a tap-to-call button matter more here than beautiful design ever will. I optimize for the sixty-second decision, not the portfolio piece.

Two businesses

Emergency jobs pay the bills. Quarterly plans build the business.

There are two businesses hiding inside every pest control company, and they need completely different content. Emergency work is panic-driven, phone-based, and decided in minutes. Recurring prevention is calm, researched, and decided over days. Most pest control sites build for one and ignore the other, which means they are leaving half their revenue on the table. I run both tracks, because the search behavior behind each one has almost nothing in common:

Page anatomy

What a treatment page needs to win the call.

A treatment page has one job: turn a panicked searcher into a caller. That means answering the four questions every pest customer has, in order. First, is this my problem: signs and identification, so the searcher self-diagnoses. Second, how do you fix it: the treatment process, in plain language, no jargon. Third, is it safe: chemicals, kids, pets, what to expect on the day. Fourth, what does it cost: honest ranges and what moves them. Pages that answer all four convert. Pages that say "we handle bed bugs, call for a quote" do not.

Safety content deserves special attention, because it is the objection that kills conversions silently. Customers will not always ask about chemicals around their children or pets. They will simply choose the company whose site already answered it. A short, honest safety section on every treatment page, what you use, why it is safe when applied correctly, what precautions to take, removes the objection before it forms. In a trade where a stranger sprays chemicals inside someone's home, this section is doing more selling than your entire about page.

Pricing is the other silent killer. You do not need fixed prices, but you do need ranges and honesty about what moves them: severity, property size, treatment type. "Bed bug treatment typically falls between X and Y depending on the infestation" beats "call for a free quote" every time, because the customer searching at midnight is comparing, and the page with numbers wins the comparison. The companies afraid to show pricing are not protecting their margins. They are donating their callers to whoever was braver.

The calendar

Pests run on a schedule. Your content should too.

Pest demand is seasonal and the seasons are predictable. Ants surge in spring. Wasps and mosquitoes own the summer. Rodents move indoors in autumn. Termite swarm season arrives like clockwork. Every year brings the same spikes, the same panicked searches, and the same competitors scrambling to publish content after the calls have already started.

The move is to publish before the spike, not during it. A wasp nest removal page published in winter is ranking by June. A rodent proofing guide published in summer owns the autumn searches. Google needs time to trust a new page, so seasonal content has a built-in lead time, and the companies that respect it capture weeks of high-intent traffic while everyone else is still writing. It is some of the highest-return content in the trade, precisely because the demand curve is public knowledge and most companies still react to it instead of preparing for it.

I map the full year for every pest control client: which pests spike when, which pages need to exist before each spike, and which existing pages need refreshing as the season approaches. A wasp nest removal page should be updated in spring with that year's availability, not left to gather dust since 2023. Seasonal SEO is a calendar discipline, and it compounds: every year the pages get stronger, the rankings get stickier, and the spikes get easier to win.

Trust

A stranger with chemicals: why reviews are the whole decision.

Pest control is a high-trust purchase from a stranger who will be inside your home spraying chemicals. Reviews are not a nice extra here; for a huge share of customers they are the entire decision. And the reviews that convert mention specifics: arrived the same day, explained everything, used a pet-safe treatment, no more mice after one visit. Vague five-star reviews do almost nothing next to a detailed one that answers the exact fear the searcher has.

I set up review requests timed to the relief moment: the day after treatment, when the customer feels the problem is handled and the gratitude is fresh. A text with a direct link, nothing more complicated. Steady, specific reviews do two jobs at once: they lift map rankings, and they close the sale for the next panicked searcher reading them at midnight. Volume matters, but specificity is what turns a rating into revenue.

Then there is the customer most pest control sites never speak to: the property manager. One relationship can mean dozens of units on quarterly plans, and property managers search differently than homeowners. They want response times, documentation, multi-unit pricing, and proof of similar accounts. I build commercial pages that speak their language, because most residential-focused competitors leave the entire commercial lane open. The same trust-first approach works across home services: the cleaning companies I work with sell the same thing, a stranger in your home, and whether you spray in one town or run routes across the USA, trust content is what turns rankings into revenue.
