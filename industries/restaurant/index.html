Friday night

The table goes to whoever Google shows first

It is Friday, 7:40 in the evening. Two people are standing on a pavement, phones out, hungry. One of them types best italian near me. Google shows three restaurants on the map. They tap the one with 4.8 stars and photos of actual pasta, not stock photography. They glance at the menu, see a table is free, and book. The whole decision takes four minutes.

Your restaurant might cook better food. It does not matter. You were not in the visible three, so for those two diners, you do not exist.

I think about that moment constantly, because it is the entire business case for SEO for restaurants. Diners do not browse anymore. They search, they scan the top three map results, they check ratings and photos, and they commit. The restaurants that win are not always the best kitchens. They are the best at being found and chosen inside a four-minute window.

That is what I build: a Google presence that puts you in the visible three, a review footprint that makes you the obvious tap, and a website that turns the tap into a booking or a direct order.

The game

Why restaurant SEO plays by its own rules

Most local SEO is about being found. Restaurant SEO is about being found and picked, in that order, within minutes. The map pack matters more here than in almost any other industry, because the search happens on a phone, often while the person is already out and already hungry. Your Google Business Profile does more selling than your website: hours, menu link, photos, reviews, the booking or order button. If the profile is thin, the website never gets a chance.

Reviews are the currency. Diners read them like a menu and trust recent ones most. A steady flow of fresh reviews lifts your map rankings and closes the sale for the next person reading at 7:40 on a Friday.

Then there is the menu problem. Google cannot read your menu from a PDF or a photo. A typed-out, indexable menu with named dishes ranks for dish-level searches, shows up in Google's menu display, and keeps visitors on your site. Your dishes are keywords. Most restaurants never publish them properly.

And then there are the apps. Delivery platforms will happily sell you your own customers back at up to 30 percent commission per order. They are fine as extra volume. As a strategy, they are a treadmill: they own the customer, the data, and the repeat order. SEO for restaurants is the way off the treadmill, whether your dining room sits on a UK high street or a Dubai food court, because a customer who finds you on Google and orders direct is yours to keep.

The hub

Same hunger, four very different searches

A restaurant is not one business on Google. It is several, and each one is searched differently. A cafe fights chains for coffee near me. A bakery wins custom birthday cake near me at 11pm on a Thursday. A food truck has to rank without a fixed address. A pizza place is in a knife fight with delivery apps over every single order.

They belong together, because the fundamentals are the same and the differences live in the details. Here is the map:

Cafes

Your cafe against the chain on the corner

When someone types coffee near me, Google shows three cafes and a map. Everything else on page one barely matters. So every engagement I do for a cafe starts with the local pack checklist, in order.

Primary category Cafe, secondary Coffee shop. Wrong categories are the most common issue I find: if Google thinks you are a restaurant, you will not rank for cafe searches. Menu linked as typed text, not just photographed, because a real menu with your signature drinks lets you rank for matcha latte near me and its friends. Hours correct, including holidays: nothing kills trust like Google saying open when you are closed. Photos weekly, drinks, interior, storefront. Your latte art is content, so post it.

Reviews get answered like a human, every one of them, especially the critical ones. Future customers read your replies as a preview of how you treat people. Amenities get listed: wifi, laptop-friendly, outdoor seating, dog-friendly are search filters, and if you have them but do not list them, you are invisible to everyone filtering for them. Google Posts cover events and seasonal menus.

And there are two customers, not one. The regular searches your name directly and cares about consistency and correct hours; win her once and she is worth hundreds of visits. The tourist searches on a phone with bad signal and walks into whichever cafe loaded first. Your own site has to outrank the directories and load fast on mobile, or the tourist never becomes a regular.

  • Category Cafe first, menu typed out, hours right on holidays.
  • Fresh photos every week. Your drinks are your content.
  • Answer every review. Future customers read replies as a preview of your service.
  • List every amenity. They are search filters, not decoration.
Bakeries

The 6am search that decides a bakery's day

It is Thursday night. Someone needs a birthday cake for Saturday, so they type custom birthday cake near me. Google shows three bakeries on the map. They tap the one with photos of actual cakes, scroll through recent reviews mentioning birthday cakes, find a gallery with a cake that looks like the one in their head, and hit an order form that takes two minutes. By Friday morning the order is in. Your competitor just sold a cake while you were asleep.

This happens every night, in every town, for every occasion. The bakery that shows up with proof wins the order. Not the cheapest, not the closest. The one whose Google presence answers can they make the cake I am imagining before the customer even calls.

And it is not just custom cakes. Sourdough near me at 7am. Croissants open now on a Saturday. Wedding cake tasting, planned three months out. Every product you bake is a search term, and every search term is a customer you either capture or hand to the bakery down the road. That is the mindset shift: your menu is already a keyword list. It just needs publishing.

Most bakery websites hide the menu in a PDF or skip it entirely. I build it properly instead: every item published with a real description and a photo, because sourdough, almond croissants, vegan bakes and corporate catering are all searches. Wedding cakes get their own page entirely, since wedding cake plus your area is a high-value search worth fighting for. The oven was already doing the work. Google just needed to be told about it.

  • Treat every menu item as a search term and publish it properly.
  • Wedding and occasion cakes get dedicated pages with real photos.
  • Reviews that mention specific products sell the next order.
  • An order form that takes two minutes beats a phone number in the footer.
Food trucks

Marketing a restaurant that moves

Every other food business has an address. You have a schedule. Monday you are outside the office park, Friday at the brewery, Saturday at a wedding. Google's local system was built for fixed locations, which means food trucks start with a structural disadvantage: the algorithm wants to know where you are, and the honest answer is, it depends on the day.

Most trucks rely on social media stories: find us today at, posted at 10am, gone by tomorrow. That reaches followers, not the office worker searching lunch near me at noon or the couple searching food truck catering six months before their wedding. Those two searches, hungry-now and planning-ahead, are the entire strategy.

Here is the system I build. Home-base location pages: your commissary kitchen, your storage yard, your most regular pitch each become a page. Google needs geographic anchors, so we give it real ones instead of pretending the truck has no home. One schedule page, where to find us this week, on the same URL, updated every week. Regulars bookmark it, Google sees fresh content weekly, and lunch searches land on a page that is always current. Event and catering pages: weddings, corporate events, festivals, each targeting food truck catering plus the event and area. These are the highest-value searches in the trade and the least competitive.

The Business Profile gets set up as a service-area business, covering your rotation with no fake address. Done wrong this gets you suspended. Done right, a truck with a smart location strategy can own lunch searches across its whole route, from office parks across the USA to weekend markets anywhere. Almost no food trucks do SEO properly, because the moving-target problem scares everyone off. The field is wide open.

  • Give Google geographic anchors: home-base pages for your real locations.
  • One schedule page, same URL, updated every single week.
  • Catering pages per event type capture the highest-value searches.
  • Set the profile up as a service-area business. Never fake an address.
Pizza

Take back the 30 percent you pay the apps

Run the numbers on your own orders and the delivery apps start looking like the most expensive marketing you do. Up to 30 percent commission on every single order. The app owns the customer, not you: no data, no remarketing. You compete side by side with every rival, sorted by who pays. Your brand buried under theirs.

Now the direct route. Zero commission, so the full margin stays with you. You own the customer data and the repeat order. Your site ranks for your name and pizza near me. Your brand, your photos, your story, your prices. The shift does not even require customers to change behavior: they already search Google for pizza. The only question is whether the order button they tap sends 30 percent to an app or 100 percent to you. That is a ranking problem, and ranking problems are solvable.

Almost every pizza site is missing the same pages. A real menu page, not a PDF: Google cannot read your menu from a PDF or an image, but a text menu with prices ranks for pepperoni pizza plus your area, vegan pizza near me, and dozens of dish searches. Delivery area pages: one per area you deliver to, naming neighborhoods and postcodes, because pizza delivery plus a neighborhood is searched constantly and barely contested. An online ordering page that converts: not a phone number buried in the footer, a proper order flow visible in one tap from Google, faster than opening a delivery app.

Then the pages competitors ignore. Catering and party orders: office lunches, birthdays, match nights, better margins. A catering page captures the planners while everyone else fights over single pizzas. And a late-night page, if you are open late: say it loudly, because pizza open now is searched by people with zero patience and full wallets.

  • A text menu page outranks any PDF and captures dish-level searches.
  • One delivery page per area you serve, naming neighborhoods.
  • Make ordering one tap from Google, faster than opening an app.
  • Catering and late-night pages catch the orders competitors ignore.
Process

What working with me actually looks like

Week one is the audit. I go through your Google profile, your site, your reviews, and your top three competitors, then show you exactly where you stand and what it would take to reach the top three. No 40-page PDF full of jargon. A clear picture and a priority list.

Then the profile and review sprint. Categories fixed, menu published as real text, photos flowing, hours right. The review engine goes on: a simple ask at the right moment, after the meal, after the cake pickup, after the delivery. Replies handled with a human voice.

Then the money pages. Your menu rebuilt as indexable content. Service and area pages for what you actually sell, whether that is wedding cakes, office catering, or late-night slices. Content that answers what diners search, and local links that earn authority instead of buying it.

Every month you get a plain-English report: what changed and what it did. Calls, bookings, direction requests, orders. Rankings matter, but revenue is the scoreboard. That is SEO for restaurants the way I do it: built for the four minutes that decide your Friday night.
